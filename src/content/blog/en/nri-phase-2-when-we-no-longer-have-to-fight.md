## And then comes minute 21…

The music changes. The rhythm that has so far carried active movement, strength, defensive reactions, coordination and spontaneity gives way to a calmer melody. The first phase of NRI comes to an end.

The client has just been through a series of movement experiences in which they could push, pull away, set a boundary, express strength, coordinate movement or follow the needs of their own body. Now they no longer have to act. A space opens up in which they can simply be.

I consider this transition one of the most important moments of the protocol. Symbolically, it is a path from actively coping with the outside world to being able to accept support, rest and closeness with another person. From the strength we have to use to the strength we no longer have to prove.

## Safety begins before getting into the sheet

The suspended sheet is not a surprise for the client. I show it to them before the session starts. I explain how to sit and lie down in it, and we try out the whole process together. That way they know their position, know what to expect and can ask for changes. Predictability can reduce uncertainty and support a sense of control.

When Édith Piaf starts playing after the song Happy, I stand up and, if needed, help the client get up. I hold the sheet open so they can sit down comfortably, and I help them settle into the position we tried beforehand. I sit down beside them, place my hand on their back between the shoulder blades with their consent, and very slowly begin rocking them forward and back.

### Édith Piaf – Non, je ne regrette rien: when something can hold us

In open space, a person carries their own weight, keeps their balance and decides how to move. In the sheet, the body is supported over a larger surface. Symbolically, this is a transition from a space where we have to act to a space where we can exist. I don’t always have to carry everything on my own. I can lean on something that will hold me – even on another person, without losing my own identity.

I explain to the client in advance that I can very gently wrap the sheet around their body. If they don’t agree, they stay in the open sheet and we continue with rocking only. If they agree, the movement is extremely slow. After the first turn, when the fabric surrounds the body more, I stop them in the starting orientation and rock them gently. Later I add more slow half-turns; in some positions I gently place one hand on the back of the head and keep up the rocking with the other. I quietly hum the melody.

Being wrapped symbolically reminds me of a mother’s arms. It is not, however, a literal return to the womb or proof of processing prenatal memories. For one person being enclosed can feel pleasant, for another threatening. Safety doesn’t come from taking control away, but from being able to decide about your own body and say no at any moment.

### Gabriela Gunčíková – I Stand: I can accept support and stay myself

I watch the client’s position, breathing, spontaneous movements and tension. I notice how they respond to closeness and touch, whether they need more space and how their experience is changing. These signs are not clear-cut diagnostic markers; they help me adjust what I do.

The contact of the fabric provides proprioceptive information about the position and boundaries of the body. Rocking engages vestibular processing of movement and balance. A calmer environment can support interoceptive awareness of breathing or heartbeat. Predictable movement can calm some people, and the sensitive presence of the therapist can support trust. These general mechanisms, however, do not on their own prove the specific effectiveness of NRI.

With experiences of suffocation, physical restraint or other traumatisation, being wrapped can trigger fear, loss of control or dissociative reactions. In that case we don’t wrap the sheet. The goal is not to endure discomfort, but to safely recognise one’s own needs and respond to them.

### Robbie Williams – Angels: closeness that asks for nothing

The gentle rocking continues, with slow turns of the sheet if the client consents, therapeutic touch and quiet humming. The client no longer has to coordinate movement or complete a task. They can just lie there.

In everyday life, contact with others often comes with expectations. Here I try to offer a different experience: being with another person without any demand to perform. Opening the eyes, changing position or declining touch are all full expressions of boundaries too.

### Lady Gaga – Hold My Hand: the experience of acceptance

This song has a personal meaning for me. When I sit beside the client and gently rock them, a feeling sometimes arises in me similar to what a mother feels holding a small child: tenderness, acceptance and care. I don’t have to want anything from the client. They don’t have to prove that the therapy works.

This is my inner therapeutic stance, not a claim about what the client is experiencing. They may feel calm, sadness, uncertainty or a need for distance. Acceptance doesn’t mean I know what the other person feels. It means I give them room to feel what they really feel.

### Alexandra Burke – Hallelujah: safety we can take with us

As this song begins, I start very slowly unwinding the sheet. Over the course of the whole song, the client gradually returns to the original open position. The space around the body grows.

Symbolically, this is a return from a supported and enclosed environment to the open world. Getting out abruptly would interrupt this transition; opening gradually gives time to sense the change in the contact of the fabric, the position of the body and the space. We can leave a safe shelter and perhaps take part of the feeling of support with us. Whether this carries over into everyday life is a question for further observation.

### The Piano Guys – O Come, O Come, Emmanuel: returning to your own body

The sheet is open and the client is lying calmly. With their consent, my hand is between their shoulder blades and I gradually slow the rocking until it stops. When the music fades, I leave a moment of silence and invite the client to sense their body, with eyes closed if that feels right for them.

Then comes a body orientation exercise: we imagine a piece of chalk slowly tracing the outline of the body. We start at the big toe of the right foot and continue over the toes, the sole, ankle, calf, knee, thigh, hips, chest, arm, shoulder, neck and head, then down the other side of the body back to the starting point. This is a conscious shifting of attention to individual parts of the body, not proof of change in specific brain structures.

Then the client gently moves their arms and legs, stretches and slowly opens their eyes. They orient themselves in the room. When they are ready, they put their feet out of the sheet onto the floor, feel their contact with the ground and sit up at their own pace. No one rushes them.

## The whole protocol as a journey

Across both phases of NRI, a symbolic arc can be seen: contact → strength → mobilisation → boundaries → coordination → release → spontaneity → support → trust → acceptance → return to oneself.

It is not a fixed path that every person has to walk the same way. Each client comes with their own life experience, individual needs and a different capacity to handle activation, closeness or rest. That is why individual songs and movements can trigger different reactions in different people. One person finds relief in intense movement, another in being able to say STOP, and yet another only at the moment when they can let themselves be rocked calmly in the sheet.

What matters is not going through all the phases in a prescribed way, but allowing a person to discover what their body needs in that moment. The sequence of songs creates a predictable structure that can support orientation and a sense of security, while staying open to individual adjustments.

The point of NRI is not to teach a person to react according to a predetermined script, but to offer them the experience of having a choice. They can activate and calm down, express strength and accept help, come closer and move away, act and rest.

And perhaps the deepest symbolism of the whole therapy lies in this very freedom: I no longer have to just react automatically to what once happened to me. I can gradually discover new ways of being in my body, in relationship with others and in the world.

## Safety and the limits of interpretation

Work in the suspended sheet must be voluntary, individually adapted and guided with ongoing consent. The secure attachment and load capacity of the sheet must be checked, a free airway ensured, along with the ability to stop immediately and get out safely. In case of dizziness, nausea, marked fear or signs of dissociation, the movement must be stopped and the response adapted to the client’s state.

The meanings of the songs and movements described here are a therapeutic concept and a symbolic interpretation. General knowledge about sensory processing, predictability and relational support is not, on its own, proof of the clinical effectiveness of the NRI protocol. Observed reactions such as crying, trembling or relief cannot automatically be taken as proof of trauma release. NRI does not replace indicated medical or psychological care.
