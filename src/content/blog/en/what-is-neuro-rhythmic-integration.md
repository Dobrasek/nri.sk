Imagine music that soothes your senses. A rhythm that sets your body moving. Movement that awakens all kinds of feelings in us. And then imagine a warm, safe embrace where you can let go for a moment and simply be.

Now imagine all of these elements coming together in one therapeutic process.

Because sometimes it isn’t enough to understand what happened to us. And sometimes we don’t even need to know exactly what it is to feel that it’s there inside us.

Imagine a moment when you’re afraid. You’ve already learned to understand your fears. You know when they come, you know your triggers, and maybe you can even name the situations in which it happens to you.

And yet you often can’t stop your reaction. Because the body can react before you even start to think about it.

Your heart starts pounding. Your body goes stiff. You want to run. You can’t say no. Or you disconnect from what you’re experiencing altogether.

It was out of this very experience that Neuro-Rhythmic Integration – NRI – gradually took shape.

NRI is an original music-and-movement therapeutic method that works with rhythm, a predictable sequence, movement, pressure and resistance, body awareness, boundaries, touch and the therapeutic relationship.

Its goal is not to teach a person to be calm all the time. The goal is to support the ability to sense your own body, to work safely with different intensities of activation, to use movement and strength, to slow down, to set a boundary and to gradually return to a more stable state.

## NRI grew out of my personal experience

NRI wasn’t created at a desk or out of a textbook. It grew out of my own experience, my curiosity and a gradual search for an answer to the question of what the body needs when understanding alone isn’t enough.

My adolescence was a time of intense anxiety, panic, fear, pain, disconnection and searching for myself.

Today I understand that I got through that period partly with the help of very strong defensive reactions. One of them was disconnection from my own experience. There were times when I felt I could hardly feel anything at all – mentally or physically.

Looking back, I can see that even this disconnection had a protective function at that time. It allowed me to function when I didn’t yet have the capacity to process everything I was carrying inside.

And yet there was a place where I felt alive, full of energy, present and myself. It was music and movement.

House music, a repetitive rhythm, darkness and the chance to move in a way where everyone stayed in their own space gave me something I didn’t yet understand back then.

The rhythm was predictable. I didn’t have to think about what would come next.

When I moved, I could suddenly feel the individual parts of my body. I sensed my muscles, my breath, the movement and the space. I felt lightness and freedom, and at the same time I was able to stay present and notice what was happening inside me.

Many years later, during my own therapeutic work, I realised that in difficult physical and emotional states I was once again seeking out similar elements: rhythm, predictability, movement, guidance, a safe relationship and tenderness.

And that is where the concept of NRI began to be born.

## From free movement to a safe structure

During one therapy training, I took part in a therapy based on free movement to music.

We were blindfolded, each of us had our own space around us, and the music was played very loud. The instruction was simple:

*“Do what your body needs.”*

But my body didn’t move. I just stood there.

Inside, I was experiencing a whole range of emotions – anger, fear, rage and joy too – but I couldn’t express them through movement.

Today I interpret this experience as my body not having a sufficient sense of safety at that moment for spontaneous movement. Instead of active expression, I stayed in a state of heightened alertness and immobility.

Even so, I left the therapy with a feeling of great relief. And a question was already running through my head:

**How can we create conditions in which a person could not only feel what is happening inside them, but gradually also express it safely through movement?**

And that is where the structure of NRI began to be born.

I realised that for a person who is in good contact with their body, the sentence “move the way you feel right now” can bring freedom.

But what about a person who can hardly feel their body? What about a person who freezes under stress? What about a person who is flooded with shame when they move? What about a person with anxiety who feels that every movement they make is wrong?

For such a person, complete freedom may not be liberating. It may just be another source of uncertainty.

That is why NRI gradually gained a firm, predictable structure.

**The structure stays. The intensity can change.**

I sat down opposite the client. I turned the music down so they could still hear me. I began offering specific movements. I created a fixed order of songs and gradually assigned movement tasks to the individual parts of the protocol.

Movements emerged that work with strength, pressure, resistance, mobilisation, stopping, boundaries, coordination, rocking and slowing down afterwards.

In this way, NRI gradually stopped being just spontaneous movement to music and began to gain its own therapeutic architecture.

One of its core principles today is:

**The structure stays. The intensity can change.**

The basic protocol lasts about 39 minutes, and the songs always follow in the same order.

The first roughly 21 minutes are the movement part. It alternates rhythm, pressure, resistance, coordination, more active movements, slowing down, and movement tasks related to boundaries and defensive reactions.

From about the 21st minute, the client moves into the second part of the protocol – work with the therapy sheet. The nature of the intervention gradually shifts towards support, contact, gentle pressure, movement, touch, voice, breath and slowing down.

A fixed sequence, however, doesn’t mean the client has to do every movement I show them. Quite the opposite.

**Movement is an offer, not an order.**

If the client doesn’t feel a movement at that moment, they don’t have to do it. They can just listen to the music, sense the rhythm and watch my movement.

That, too, gradually builds relationship, predictability and an experience of safety.

The client can make the movement bigger or smaller. They can use more strength or less. They can slow down. They can stay with just a gentle rocking. And they can say: STOP.

Sometimes the first step is simply to stay, listen to the music and start to sense the rhythm. That, too, is a movement towards your own body.

## The body doesn’t only need to know how to “calm down”

In NRI, we don’t start from the idea that a well-regulated person is someone who is calm all the time.

Life also asks us to be able to mobilise energy.

We need to be able to stand up for ourselves. Move away from danger. Say no. Use strength. Stop. Rest. Accept help.

And after mobilising, we need to be able to return to a more stable state.

That’s why NRI doesn’t work only with slowing down. It works with moving between different bodily states.

One moment we use strength. Then we let go. We speed up. We slow down. We move. We stop. We notice.

And depending on what the body can manage and needs at that moment, we carry on.

## The intellectual roots of NRI

In creating NRI, I was influenced by a number of authors and therapeutic approaches dealing with trauma, attachment, bodily experience, emotions and autonomic regulation.

In his affective neuroscience, Jaak Panksepp described evolutionarily ancient emotional-motivational systems linked, among other things, to seeking, care, play, fear and anger.

John Bowlby’s attachment tradition, in turn, brought the concept of the secure base – a relationship from which we can explore the world and to which we can return for support.

Other important intellectual sources of NRI for me are the works of Peter A. Levine, Bruce D. Perry, Bessel van der Kolk, Stephen Porges, Gabor Maté, Matthew Appleton, Arthur Janov, Guy Corneau and Laurence Heller.

NRI, however, is not identical to any of their methods.

It is an independent, original synthesis that grew out of connecting theoretical knowledge, my personal experience, therapeutic practice and the gradual observation of clients’ reactions.

And that is exactly why I want to continue researching, measuring and developing NRI systematically.

## NRI is not a path to a body that is always calm

It is work on the ability to stay in contact with ourselves even when our bodily and emotional state is changing.

To feel activation. To use strength. To slow down. To set a boundary. To accept support. To say STOP. And to come back again to a more stable state.

Perhaps the whole idea of NRI can be summed up in three simple questions:

**What am I feeling right now?**

**What do I need right now?**

**And what can I do about it?**
