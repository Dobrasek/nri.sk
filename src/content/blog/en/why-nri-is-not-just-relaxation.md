When people hear “nervous system regulation”, many of them picture calm.

Slow breathing. Relaxed muscles. Silence. Relaxation.

That can be part of regulation too. But no one can live their whole life in calm alone.

Sometimes we need to slow down. Other times we need to stand up. Get going. Defend ourselves. Say no. Use strength. Leave a situation that is hurting us. Or, on the contrary, stay where we once would have automatically run away from.

So for me, a regulated person is not someone who is calm all the time.

It’s someone who can change their state according to what the situation requires, without losing contact with themselves.

And that is exactly why NRI is not just relaxation.

## When more calming isn’t what we need

Imagine a person who is tired, low on energy, disconnected from their body, barely responsive to their surroundings, and who freezes in difficult situations.

Do they need to slow down even more?

Maybe not.

Maybe their body first needs to experience that it can generate energy and use it safely.

This was one of the important realisations behind NRI.

I knew very well the states in which I didn’t need another “calm down”. I needed to feel that I had strength. That I could move. That I could push. That I could say no. That I didn’t have to automatically freeze or run away.

That’s why NRI includes pressure, resistance, rhythmic movement, movements resembling running or boxing, and also a clear STOP gesture.

The point isn’t to stir a person up as much as possible.

The point is to let them meet their own energy in a dose they can handle at that moment.

## Activation is not the same as overwhelm

This is a crucial difference.

Activation can mean that movement speeds up, breathing changes, the person feels more energy, uses more strength, or an emotion comes up.

Overwhelm is something else.

That’s why in NRI I don’t only watch whether the client is doing the movement. I watch how present they stay while doing it.

I notice their breath, face, eyes, muscle tension, contact, their ability to respond to me and, above all, their own signals.

We can make the movement smaller. Slow it down. Reduce the pressure. Switch to rocking. Stop. Or stay with just the music.

In somatically oriented approaches, this principle is often described with the term titration – working with intense experience in small, manageable doses. In NRI, titration is one of the practical principles for dosing movement, pressure and activation.

Research in the broader field of emotion regulation also shows links between the perception of internal bodily signals, HRV and the ability to regulate emotional reactions more flexibly, although this cannot automatically be taken as evidence of NRI’s effectiveness.

## And what if a person can’t move?

Then I don’t force them.

For me, this is one of the most important principles of NRI.

If a deeply frozen client just sits through the first session, listening to the music and watching my movements, that too is a beginning.

First they may start to sense the rhythm. Then the vibrations of the music. Maybe a gentle rocking will come. Later a small movement of the hand. And only in time, greater activity.

For a person who has protected themselves for years through immobility or disconnection, the goal doesn’t have to be to “break through the freeze” right away. What matters more is that they have the experience:

**I don’t have to move because someone expects it of me. I can move when I’m ready.**

## The first 21 minutes: the body discovers different possibilities

The first part of the basic NRI protocol lasts about 21 minutes.

It isn’t designed for the intensity to just keep rising. Even within it, the nature of the movement, strength, rhythm and bodily experience change.

We start with Queen – We Will Rock You. The rhythm is strong and predictable. We work with pressure and strength.

Next comes The Prodigy – No Good, with more active movement and working with strength across the body’s midline.

With Rammstein – Du Hast, we work with pulling and resistance.

Then come Imagine Dragons – Demons, where more active movement can alternate with rocking, and Linkin Park – In the End, where we use the “take and throw away” movement across the centre of the body.

With Evanescence – Bring Me to Life, the nature of the movement changes once more.

Next comes My Immortal, where we slow down more noticeably and work with bending forward, bending back and a calmer bodily experience.

And the first phase closes with Pharrell Williams – Happy – rhythm, coordination, contact and an element of play.

So it isn’t: activation → activation → even more activation.

It’s more like: activation → change → noticing → more activation → slowing down → movement again.

It is this very movement between states that matters in NRI.

## After the 21st minute, the nature of the therapy changes

After Happy, the client moves into the therapy sheet.

Édith Piaf – Non, je ne regrette rien begins.

Here the nature of the whole process changes. The active movement part steps back and support, being wrapped, gentler movement and contact come to the fore.

Next comes Gabriela Gunčíková – I Stand, then Robbie Williams – Angels and Lady Gaga – Hold My Hand.

The client stays in the sheet and the work gradually slows down.

With Alexandra Burke – Hallelujah, we begin to gradually release the sheet.

The last song is The Piano Guys – O Come, O Come, Emmanuel. By now the client can simply lie there, sensing their breath, their bodily sensations and whatever remains from the previous part of the protocol.

For me, this second phase is not a reward for “performance” in the first part. It is the other pole of the experience.

A moment ago I was using strength. Now I can accept support.

A moment ago I was moving. Now I can stay.

A moment ago I was pushing. Now I can let go.

## Pendulation: I don’t have to stay in one state

We call this principle pendulation – moving between different bodily states.

Tension ↔ release. Activation ↔ slowing down. Strength ↔ support. Movement ↔ stillness.

For me, its significance doesn’t lie in having to get rid of a difficult feeling immediately.

A person may first notice: “I’m activated right now.”

Then: “Something has changed.”

And later they may discover: “I can come close to activation again and I don’t have to get stuck in it.”

That is a very different experience from trying to be calm all the time.

## “I must” was my inner rule for a long time

I know this part of NRI very well from my own life.

For a long time I functioned like this: I must hold on. I must keep going. I must cope. I can’t stop.

In my own therapeutic work, I began to connect this inner command with my personal story and very early experiences. That is my subjective interpretation – not something that could be objectively proven in hindsight.

But the bodily feeling was very real:

**If I stop, something bad will happen.**

So for me, the biggest change wasn’t learning to relax even better.

It was discovering:

**I can use strength and then stop.**

I can get going and then slow down. I can refuse something. I can stay. I can leave. I can change my mind.

And nothing bad has to happen just because I stopped obeying an old inner command.

## Having a choice is part of the therapy

That’s why it matters so much in NRI that the client can say: more, less, slower, enough, STOP.

They can do the movement I show them. They can change it. Or they don’t have to do it at all.

For a person who was used to adapting, suppressing their boundaries or automatically giving others what they want, even a small experience like this can be significant:

**The therapist offered something. I said no. And our relationship stayed safe.**

That’s why I don’t see NRI as training in obediently performing movements.

Quite the opposite.

Movement is the means through which a person can explore their reactions, intensity, boundaries and choices.

## Progress doesn’t have to look like calm

I learned this from clients, too.

Sometimes a client doesn’t say after therapy: “I’m calmer.”

They might say: “I feel more energy.” “I feel more alive.” “I feel anger.” “For the first time I feel like saying no.”

Or they say: “Nothing has changed.”

And yet over the following sessions I notice that they make more eye contact, communicate more, allow themselves to change a movement, try less to do everything “right”, are able to speak up, or say STOP for the first time.

For me, that can be change too.

Not because every activation automatically means therapeutic progress. But because we can’t judge regulation only by how calm a person looks.

The scientific literature on emotion regulation also works with flexibility of response rather than with the idea of a single, constant ideal state; HRV research further shows that vagal regulation can change both during stress and during subsequent recovery.

## Emotions aren’t just there to calm us

Panksepp’s affective neuroscience is also an important inspiration for me in this respect.

Panksepp described primary emotional-motivational systems such as SEEKING, RAGE, FEAR, CARE, PANIC/SADNESS and PLAY (along with the LUST system). He didn’t describe emotions merely as inner feelings, but as systems closely tied to motivation and action.

Fear can prepare the organism for a protective response.

Anger can be linked to mobilisation and the defence of resources or boundaries.

SEEKING is linked to energy directed towards exploring and obtaining resources.

PLAY to movement, social contact and joy.

NRI doesn’t follow directly from Panksepp’s theory, and I don’t claim that individual songs “activate” specific Pankseppian brain systems. His work does, however, help me think about emotions not only as something to be dampened, but also as states connected with energy, motivation and readiness to act.

## So what is NRI not?

NRI is not ordinary relaxation.

Nor is it a dance class.

It isn’t an exercise in which the client has to perform a routine correctly.

And I don’t see it as simply “letting emotions out” either.

It is a structured music-and-movement process in which different intensities and qualities of movement alternate, and the client is given space to notice:

What is happening to me?

How much is enough right now?

Do I want to continue?

Do I need to slow down?

Do I want to use strength?

Do I want to stop?

That is exactly why, for me, the essence of NRI is regulatory flexibility, not permanent calm.

## You don’t have to anymore. You can.

Perhaps we once had to adapt.

We had to stay quiet.

We had to hold on.

We had to run away.

We had to freeze.

But a strategy that was once useful doesn’t have to be the only option forever.

An adult can gradually gain a wider repertoire of responses.

They can use strength.

They can slow down.

They can stay.

They can leave.

They can accept help.

They can say no.

They can say STOP.

So for me, regulation doesn’t mean I will never experience an inner storm again.

It means that when the storm comes, I don’t have to automatically lose contact with myself.

And perhaps the simplest message of NRI is:

**You don’t have to anymore. You can.**
