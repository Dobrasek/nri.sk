A young person sits across from me. He is talking about an event that changed his life profoundly. His voice is calm, his face almost expressionless. After a while he says: “I know I should be sad. But I don’t feel anything.” I don’t ask him why he isn’t crying. What interests me is what it is like for him to live with this state, what he misses and what he himself would like to change.

In my work I meet people who seem calm or emotionally distant on the outside. Sometimes, though, their body speaks a different language: shoulders drawn up, jaw clenched, hands in fists, a shy gaze. Others seem fully functional. At work they can keep a cool head, handle crisis situations and meet their obligations. Only at home do they find they have no energy even for things they once loved.

I hear sentences like: “I live as if in armour.” “Nothing can move me.” “I do everything on autopilot.” “It’s as if I lived on another planet.” I take words like these seriously. But they don’t automatically point to a single diagnosis, or mean that the person actually feels emotions and is just hiding them. It may be emotional numbing, dissociative experiences, depressive symptoms, exhaustion or a combination of several causes.

## A quiet body isn’t necessarily resting

I explain to clients the difference between rest and a state in which the body seems to stop, while the person stays inwardly on guard. I use a simple aid of three zones – heightened activation, relative safety and shutdown – and also a description of possible defensive reactions: orienting to the threat, flight, fight, freeze or collapse. It is not a literal map of the brain, nor a fixed sequence every person has to go through. It is a way of talking about different reactions to stress.

In freeze, movement may be limited, reactions delayed and decisions hard to make. Later, the person replays in their head everything they could have said or done. But in that moment, they couldn’t respond. This is not a weakness of character. At the same time, not every stillness or slowness can be labelled a traumatic reaction; we always also consider sleep, medication, and neurological, psychiatric and metabolic causes.

## When sensitivity becomes something to hide

In clients’ histories I find stories of being shamed, ridiculed, punished for crying or anger, but also of loss, separation and betrayed trust. Some learned to always be nice, obliging and grateful. Outwardly they never say anything negative, even though inside they are experiencing tension or anger. It is possible that this way of behaving was once the safest strategy available.

When thinking about development, I find it helpful to ask questions drawn from Heller’s model of needs and Bowlby’s attachment theory: Did the person have someone with them who recognised their needs? Could they rely on help? Were they allowed to disagree? Could they stay in contact even when they were sad or angry? I use these frameworks for conversation and forming hypotheses, not as a tool that traces a single symptom back to an exact cause in childhood.

Some people long for closeness but fear it at the same time. Others feel that help won’t come anyway, so they don’t even ask for it. It’s important not to lock a person into the label “avoidant” or “anxious”. Attachment behaviour changes with the situation, with experience and with how safe a particular relationship is.

## Safety is not an order: “Now you must feel”

When a client starts focusing on the body, sensations may come up that are new and unsettling for them. Sometimes they want to return quickly to the familiar numbness. Then I remind them: “You can stop at any time. Try only as much as feels bearable for you right now.” There’s no need to break through to the hardest memory or to bring on tears for the session to be meaningful.

In the therapeutic relationship, what matters to me is naturalness, genuine interest and clear boundaries. The client can experience acceptance without having to perform or meet the therapist’s expectations. That is exactly why they must have the right to refuse touch, movement, closing their eyes and the intervention itself. The relationship should strengthen their independence, not create another dependence.

If I notice disorientation, panic or loss of contact with the surroundings, I don’t invite the person to go deeper into the emotion. I help them orient themselves in the present: “Look around you. What do you see? Which object helps you realise where you are? What do you need right now?” If overwhelm persists, we stop the intervention and, if needed, arrange further professional help.

## NRI: movement as an offer, not a test

In Activation NRI we begin with orienting in the space and moving to the rhythm of the music. I show the movements, and sometimes do them together with the client if they agree. I watch their pace, orientation, ability to communicate and whether they want to continue. When it gets to be too much, we slow down. If they don’t want to do a movement, they can just watch. Even a refusal is valuable information about their needs.

A particularly important moment comes when a person says: “I don’t want this.” “I need a break.” “Today I’d like to try it differently.” I respect that kind of answer. The goal is not to teach the client to imitate the therapist correctly, but to support their ability to sense their own boundaries and make decisions about themselves.

In regulation-oriented NRI we work with slower movement, body awareness, music and, if the client wishes, the sheet and touch. If they don’t feel comfortable closing their eyes, they keep them open. If they don’t want touch, I don’t touch them. For some people, slowing down can support contact with the body; for others, it can be unpleasant or deepen disconnection. That’s why I don’t consider these variants a universal recipe, and depending on the response we choose a different approach.

## What HRV and EEG show – and what they don’t

I observe different physiological profiles in clients. Some say they feel calm even though they have a higher heart rate or a different HRV pattern. Others describe high stress even though the measurement shows no corresponding change. This kind of mismatch is not proof that the client “isn’t really stressed” or doesn’t know how to feel properly. HRV is influenced by breathing, sleep, medication, health and the measurement conditions, and it is not a direct test for traumatisation.

With EEG, I look at how the rhythms react with eyes open and closed. But even an unusual pattern has to be assessed in light of age, alertness, artefacts and clinical context. EEG alone cannot determine that a person has frozen or has suppressed emotions. That’s why, in our pilot monitoring, we want to connect the measurements with what clients themselves describe: sleep, motivation, the ability to rest, coping with anxiety and functioning in everyday life.

## A change that shows up only outside the practice

I remember a client who described a lack of motivation and a feeling that he couldn’t get himself going. After a session, his partner wrote to me that he had started creating again, writing and planning new projects. It is an encouraging experience from practice, not proof that NRI alone caused the change. What matters is tracking whether similar changes recur, how long they last and whether the client himself finds them beneficial.

Sometimes returning to more intense experiencing is unpleasant at first. We talk about what the person feels, where they sense it in the body and how they can dose their contact with this experience. We don’t measure success by the number of tears, the intensity of anger or whether a big emotional reaction follows the therapy. What matters more is whether their ability to function, feel safer and make their own decisions is improving.

## You don’t have to prove anything

To a person who tells me “I feel nothing,” I don’t want to promise that their protective armour will surely melt away. I want to tell them that their experience deserves respect, that they don’t have to pretend anything, and that there are ways to explore their difficulties professionally and work with them gradually. If emotional numbing is connected to trauma, psychotherapy with proven effectiveness can also be part of wider care.

For me, what matters is this: a person doesn’t have to cry for their sadness to be real. They don’t have to be loudly angry for their boundaries to have value. And they don’t have to feel a certain way during therapy to deserve acceptance. The goal of NRI is not to make a person feel. It is an offer of a safe space in which they can gradually explore their experience, their boundaries and their ways of responding.

*Note: NRI is a therapeutic approach still in development; its effectiveness for dissociation, trauma-related disorders and emotional numbing has not yet been sufficiently demonstrated by controlled clinical studies. The cases described are experiences from practice, not proof of causal effectiveness.*
