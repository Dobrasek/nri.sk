## Introduction: Safety begins with having a choice

Imagine you come to therapy expecting precise instructions. Instead, the therapist asks you where you’d like to settle, what light suits you and where they themselves should be. For someone who has spent their whole life adapting to others, even this can be an unusual experience.

In NRI, therapeutic work doesn’t begin with the first song. It begins with respect for what the client needs, wants and refuses. The therapist is not just someone who plays music and shows movements. They are a guide who also carries professional responsibility for the session running safely.

## Before the first note of music

Before the session, I talk with the client about how they are right now: about sleep, energy, tension, restlessness, medications they take and circumstances that may affect their reactions. As part of a pilot observation, I also take a five-minute HRV measurement. This measurement is supplementary physiological information; on its own it doesn’t determine whether a person is safe or ready for therapy.

In the room, the client chooses the mat, the spot and the distance between us. I show them the movements and the option of working in the sheet in advance. They decide on the music volume, the lighting, eye contact and whether they want verbal guidance. They can skip a movement, just listen or ask for a break at any time.

I also explain that consent to touch and movement can be withdrawn at any time. The client doesn’t have to adapt to the protocol at any cost; the way we work should adapt to their capacities.

## The therapist observes, but doesn’t judge

During the movement phase, I watch posture, breathing, coordination, muscle tension, facial expression, rhythm and contact. I’m also interested in whether the person gradually allows themselves to express their own needs. Sometimes a client who agreed to everything at the beginning later says: “I don’t want this. I don’t feel good here. I need it differently.”

Visible crying, anger or complete calm, however, don’t have one universal meaning. When someone clenches their fists, it doesn’t have to be anger; when they are silent, it doesn’t have to be disconnection. The therapist can observe and ask sensitively, but should not impose an interpretation of the client’s experience on them.

Some clients even tell me that the song Happy annoys them: they no longer want to be smiling all the time, they want the right to experience the full range of emotions. That too can be an important naming of their own needs.

## Show the movement, help – and then step back

In the first sessions I explain the movements, demonstrate them and slow them down as needed. If the client agrees, I can briefly guide their hands. First we look for a manageable movement, and only then for its timing to the rhythm. In later sessions I ask whether they still need commentary or would rather move on their own.

When a client apologises for mistakes or strives for perfection, I remind them: “This isn’t about perfection, but about naturalness, spontaneity and having a choice.” The point is not to produce a flawless performance, but to support autonomy.

## Touch and the sheet: support only with consent

In NRI, touch can be part of the work during pushing, pulling or in the sheet. Before every new form of contact, I explain where, how and why I will touch, and check for consent. When working in the sheet, this might be contact between the shoulder blades, at the ankles or on the head. Even after consent has been given, I watch for signs of discomfort and stop the touch if I’m in doubt.

Some people just want to lie in the sheet without touch. Others prefer that I touch only the fabric. Neither option is worse. The sheet, rocking and the therapist’s closeness are not automatically calming for everyone.

## What the therapist deliberately does not do

“Does not judge, shame, control, force, expect, assume, criticise or mock.” This sentence captures an essential part of my approach.

During movement, I don’t ask for a detailed description of a traumatic event, and I don’t try to bring the client to tears or catharsis. If memories or images come up, we can talk about them if the client wants to and it is safe. However, we need to know the relevant medical and psychological history before the work begins.

Somatically oriented approaches use the terms resourcing, titration and pendulation: finding support, working with small, manageable doses of difficult experience and returning to stabilising experiences. These are working principles, not a guarantee that trauma will automatically be processed.

## Presence doesn’t mean passivity

The client may know in advance that they are allowed to leave, stop the music or ask for help. In a state of marked overwhelm, however, they may not be able to ask for help on their own. That’s why the therapist continuously monitors safety and, at signs of panic, disorientation, unresponsiveness, dissociation or physical difficulties, actively offers a pause, orientation in the room and appropriate support.

A good therapist is not the one who can trigger the strongest emotion. It is a person who can stay present and also knows when it is necessary to step in.

## The therapist’s voice and their own boundaries

In the first, active phase I use a clearer voice and concrete instructions. In the second phase I soften my voice, sometimes just humming quietly. I guide the closing body awareness with a calm voice. The message is simple: “I’m here. I see you. I sense you. If you need something, you can tell me.”

The therapist, too, may feel emotions when the client is going through something intense. Authenticity, however, doesn’t mean transferring one’s own experiences onto the client. Professional self-regulation, boundaries, supervision and knowing the limits of one’s competence are part of safe work.

## When the client is a child

With children, especially non-verbal children or children with ASD, it is often most important to first get to know the space and the therapist. There may be cushions, blankets, a tent or a sheet in the room. The child can watch, leave and come back; movement is offered gradually and without pressure.

For children I also use a shorter, roughly 22-minute version of NRI. The length, however, must match the tolerance of the particular child. Non-verbal refusal isn’t always clear-cut: it can show as stiffening, turning away, escaping, a change in breathing or loss of contact. Absence of protest is not consent.

I don’t see a child only through a diagnosis. They need to be accepted as a person, without any obligation to perform. It is precisely respecting their boundaries that can create room for cooperation.

## Sometimes calm presence is what helps

I remember a client who for a long time couldn’t settle in the sheet. She kept changing position and looking for her place. I didn’t rush to explain her experience. I stayed present, monitored her state and gave her time, as long as it was safe. Later she was able to name what she was experiencing and also described how she had noticed similar feelings at home.

This story is a personal observation, not proof of the effectiveness of NRI. It is a reminder, though, that therapeutic help doesn’t always have to mean another instruction or technique.

## Safety boundaries and contraindications of NRI

The following principles are not a validated list of contraindications for NRI; the effectiveness and safety of the whole protocol have not yet been sufficiently verified by controlled clinical studies.

### Psychiatric contraindications of NRI

NRI should not be carried out with clients in an acute, untreated or unstabilised psychiatric state. This applies in particular to the following situations:

- acute psychosis or current psychotic symptoms;
- schizophrenia in an acute or unstabilised phase;
- a severe depressive episode, especially with suicidal thoughts;
- bipolar disorder during a manic, hypomanic, mixed or severe depressive episode;
- other acute psychiatric states in which reality testing, behavioural stability or the ability to cooperate safely is impaired.

A psychiatric diagnosis on its own need not be a permanent contraindication. In a stabilised state, an individual professional assessment is needed, ideally in cooperation with the treating psychiatrist. In case of immediate risk of suicide or serious danger, the priority is urgent medical help, not NRI.

This list represents the method’s preventive safety rules. These are not clinically validated contraindications: the safety of the complete NRI protocol for individual psychiatric diagnoses has not yet been systematically verified. NRI does not replace psychiatric treatment or psychotherapy with proven effectiveness.

Before starting, it is necessary to individually assess the person’s physical and mental health, medications, capacity for informed consent, and tolerance of music, movement, touch and positioning in the sheet. In case of acute psychological deterioration, marked disorientation, psychotic or manic symptoms, or immediate risk of self-harm or violence, the priority is medical examination and stabilisation, not starting NRI.

Movement limitations, cervical spine pain, dizziness, balance disorders, cardiovascular or neurological conditions and marked sensory sensitivity may require adapting or leaving out parts of the protocol and, depending on the situation, the approval of the treating professional. The safety of rocking, rotation and work in the sheet must be assessed separately, including the risk of falling, nausea and unpleasant vestibular reactions.

During a session, reasons to stop immediately include, for example, chest pain, collapse, marked shortness of breath, a sudden neurological change, a seizure, serious disorientation or unmanageable panic. With urgent symptoms, immediate medical help must be arranged.

Every touch, movement and change of position requires appropriate explanation and ongoing consent. With children, in addition to the consent of the legal guardian, the child’s own signs of consent and refusal must also be respected. Darkness must not prevent safe monitoring and orientation.

NRI is not meant to replace indicated psychiatric, psychological, neurological or other medical care. Specific safety procedures and contraindications need further professional development.

## What the professional literature tells us

The therapeutic alliance is linked to the quality of cooperation and to outcomes in several forms of psychotherapy, but this finding on its own does not confirm the effectiveness of NRI. A trauma-informed approach emphasises safety, trust, collaboration, choice and the prevention of retraumatisation.

Co-regulation refers to a process in which the relationship with another person and their responses can support coping with stress. It is, however, neither automatic nor the same for everyone. Non-verbal signals are a valuable part of communication, but they are not a reliable tool for reading hidden emotions.

Professional boundaries include consent, competence, risk assessment, protection of confidentiality and the ability to refer on to further professional care.

## Conclusion: A guide, not a director

“An NRI therapist is a guide, a teacher, a support, an acceptance and a new direction.” This is how I see my role. I don’t have to decide for the client what they should feel and when they should change. I can offer them structure, support and a respectful space in which they gradually discover their own possibilities.

The most important thing is not that the client keeps needing me. What matters is that they gradually get better at recognising their needs, can express their boundaries and are able to draw on their own resources.

Safe therapeutic presence is therefore not inactivity. It is attentive, professional and respectful work that knows when to accompany, when to step back and when to step in.
