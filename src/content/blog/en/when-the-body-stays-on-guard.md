## When we finally stop, but calm doesn’t come

Imagine an evening after a demanding day. You finally sit down on the sofa. Everything is done, nobody wants anything from you. And yet you can’t rest. More tasks start racing through your head, your legs move restlessly, and you might rather get up to do one more thing. When someone tells you, “Come on, just rest,” your tension rises even more.

I come across similar experiences in clients of NRI – deep brain structures therapy. They tell me: “I keep thinking. I can’t switch off. I can’t fall asleep. I’m explosive. I can’t lie down and do nothing.” Others describe disconnection from their emotions, distractibility or unpleasant feelings in the company of other people.

In some, the tension is visible: they check their watch, avoid eye contact, breathe fast, can’t sit still. Others seem calm on the outside, but when asked, they admit to strong inner tension, stomach aches or pressure in the chest. That’s why I don’t rely only on first impressions. I ask how the person really feels.

## The body on alert

The autonomic nervous system continuously adjusts the activity of the heart, breathing and other bodily processes to our current needs. Under stress, activation naturally rises. Once it’s over, we need the chance to recover. Some people, however, describe the transition into rest as extremely hard for them.

I sometimes draw clients a simple map of three coloured zones. Red stands for heightened alertness, green for relative calm and manageable activation, blue for shutdown or withdrawal. It’s a useful metaphor, not an exact map of the brain or a diagnostic test. No zone is bad in itself. The goal isn’t to stay calm all the time, but to respond appropriately to stress and then be able to return to rest.

Heightened activation can be linked to long-term stress, unpredictable relationships, painful medical experiences or traumatic events. But it is not automatically proof of trauma. Similar signs can also have medical causes, including pain, lack of sleep, side effects of medication, caffeine or hormonal disorders. That’s why I ask about health, sleep, medication and other difficulties. If there are warning signs, I recommend a medical examination without waiting for several therapy sessions.

## What have we learned about our needs?

Early childhood often comes up in our conversations. Some people had to watch the moods of the adults around them, anticipate conflicts or hide their own emotions. Others were left alone with their fear or sadness. When exploring their experiences, I find it helpful to use the developmental themes described by Laurence Heller: connection, attunement to needs, trust and autonomy.

Sometimes I also use MOZGOSTROM – BRAINTREE, a drawing technique with personal colour associations. It helps us guide the conversation, not diagnose developmental trauma from colours. Finding out what the drawing means to the client matters more than interpreting it for them.

In my work I keep coming back to simple questions: Can you recognise what you need? Do you allow yourself to ask for it? Can you accept help? Can you say no? Some people know their need but don’t allow themselves to express it. Others can’t name it yet. This is exactly where building trust begins.

## Why “just lie down” sometimes doesn’t help

A person who feels inwardly threatened may not tolerate calm well. When they lie down and close their eyes, their restlessness may become more pronounced. Some clients say relaxation music doesn’t calm them at all. A simple, structured task with a clear beginning and end, on the other hand, suits them: they know what to do and don’t have to come up with their own movement.

That’s why I don’t always start with an invitation to relax. Sometimes it’s more acceptable to start with movement. This isn’t a universal rule: what decides is the client’s health, their preferences, their consent and their tolerance in the moment.

## Activation NRI: rhythm, movement and boundaries

In Activation NRI we use music and predictable movement sequences. The client can follow my movement and copy it, with no pressure to be perfect. Some especially enjoy the dynamic sequence to Rammstein. To Imagine Dragons, we alternately stretch out the right and left arm with the palm facing forward – the STOP gesture, which symbolically marks out personal space. Anyone who wants to can also say the word STOP out loud.

To Evanescence – Bring Me to Life, we work with the lotus flower movement. Clients sometimes say that at first they felt as if hidden or armoured, and then they took this protection off. This is their own symbolic experience, not a proven effect on a specific part of the brain.

I notice that some people at first just perform the movements, but gradually start to enjoy them more. Throughout the whole session, though, I watch their breathing, orientation, facial expression and signs of overload. The client can stop at any time. If panic, disorientation or marked physical discomfort appear, we actively slow down or stop the intervention; we can’t just wait to see whether the client asks for help themselves.

## Regulation NRI: when the client wants a slower path

It was clients themselves who led me to the idea of Regulation NRI. Some asked for just the calmer part: gentle movements, relaxation music and the sheet. So I began developing a different protocol, whose aim is meant to be a calm but alert presence – not numbness or disconnection from experience.

What I have in mind is slow, mindful, dosed movement during which a person can notice small changes in the body. The first part can take place sitting or lying down, depending on the client’s preference; I am considering pieces by Mei-lan as the musical accompaniment. This can then be followed by time in the sheet, gentle rocking, music and possibly therapeutic touch.

But neither the sheet, nor closed eyes, nor touch may ever be compulsory. If a stimulus doesn’t suit someone, we need the option to change it or refuse it. Being able to keep one’s eyes closed does not by itself prove safety. What matters is what the client is actually experiencing and how they tolerate the intervention.

## HRV: one piece of the puzzle

Before and after NRI we measure heart rate variability (HRV) and ask about anxiety, tension, calm, safety and energy. Sometimes heart rate, SDNN or RMSSD change. Other times the client describes relief even though HRV doesn’t show a clear increase. Subjective experience and HRV are not the same measure. The results are influenced by breathing, movement, posture, signal quality and health. A single value neither confirms nor rules out anxiety or trauma.

Over time, I would like to compare Activation and Regulation NRI. I’m interested not only in the immediate change in HRV, but also in whether the client sleeps better, is able to rest, recognises their needs and copes with difficult situations. It may turn out that some people do better starting slowly, others with movement, or with a combination of both. For now this is a research question, not a proven decision key.

## When the change shows up in everyday life

One client described a conversation with her son’s teacher. She was able to say, “STOP, now I’m talking.” She stated her position and was proud of herself. In her own words, before NRI she would have backed down.

Another client talked about speaking in public. Before, when giving talks, she would get stomach aches, a tight throat and stage fright. At her next talk after completing the sessions, by her own assessment, she didn’t feel these difficulties.

Stories like these matter as clients’ experiences. On their own, however, they don’t prove that NRI caused the change. That’s why we want to track whether similar results recur, last and show up in other situations.

## Calm is not how we look

A person can seem composed on the outside and be experiencing fear inside. And they can be energetic, moving about, and still feel safe. So I don’t want to teach people just to look calm. I want to offer them space to get to know their own needs, possibilities and boundaries.

I often say that some reactions may have helped us at a certain time in our lives. But when they remain our only way of coping with stress, they can limit us. New possibilities don’t appear on command. We can explore them gradually and try them out in safe conditions.

When someone asks me what I would say to a person who no longer knows what calm looks like, I would answer: You don’t have to force yourself to relax. We can first find out what helps you feel safer and what is manageable for you.

Calm isn’t about not coming across as nervous to others. Calm is also about whether I can sense my own needs – not just other people’s expectations.

And my personal message stays simple: I want to live, not just survive. I don’t have to anymore. Now I want to.

*Professional note: NRI is an experiential approach still in development. Its effectiveness in treating trauma or anxiety disorders has not yet been sufficiently verified by controlled research. It does not replace diagnosis, medical care or indicated psychotherapy.*
