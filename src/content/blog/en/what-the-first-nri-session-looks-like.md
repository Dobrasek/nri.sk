## Introduction

“Choose a spot where you’ll feel comfortable. And then tell me where you’d like me to be.” With this simple sentence, one of the most important parts of the first NRI session begins. No music is playing yet, we haven’t started moving, and the client isn’t lying in the sheet. Still, something essential is already happening: the person is given the chance to decide what suits them. That’s why the first meeting doesn’t start with the therapy itself, but with a conversation and with creating a space where the person doesn’t have to prove anything.

## First, we get to know each other

I first welcome the client in the room with the diagnostic devices and questionnaires. We sit down and talk about what is troubling them, why they came and what they expect. They don’t have to tell me their whole life story. But I do need to know about health and safety circumstances: medication, sleep, psychiatric diagnoses, anxiety, panic attacks and previous therapies. Sometimes I use MOZGOSTROM – BRAINTREE, a drawing technique for talking about emotions and self-perception. It is not a diagnostic test for uncovering trauma. The initial conversation takes roughly twenty to sixty minutes.

## So what is NRI, actually?

I explain to the client that NRI works with music, rhythm, movement, vibration and touch. In the first phase we focus on active movement, in the second on slowing down, rocking and rest. They don’t need to know how to dance, have a good sense of rhythm or talk about their traumatic experiences. The aim is to create space for noticing the body, tension and one’s own needs. The effectiveness of NRI in treating psychological trauma has not yet been sufficiently scientifically verified; it does not replace medical care or professional psychotherapy.

## What does the heart tell us before therapy?

I ask how the client slept, whether they’ve eaten, had coffee or alcohol, and whether they’ve taken any medication. They rate their anxiety, tension, safety, calm and energy on a scale from 1 to 10. Then comes a five-minute HRV measurement in a comfortable armchair. Heart rate variability shows the natural fluctuation of the intervals between heartbeats, which is also influenced by breathing, movement and health. It won’t tell us what trauma a person has been through. If the client doesn’t feel well, we adjust the session, postpone it or recommend further examination.

## Behind the door is a slightly different world

The room has no windows and is panelled in wood. A sheet hangs from the ceiling. On the floor there are soft mats and cushions, and in the corners a beanbag and a tent with blankets. There are weighted aids, plush emotions, a drum, tuning forks and a big speaker whose vibrations children love to explore. The light can be dimmed and its colour changed. The client can look around at everything, touch the aids and choose a spot. I ask where they want me to be, and what music volume and lighting they prefer. If they don’t know, I offer options. They can change anything at any time.

## We try out the sheet before we begin

The client sits or lies down in the sheet and tries out the rocking and, if they wish, slow turning. I show them where I might touch them and check that they consent. They can refuse or stop touch, movement or being in the sheet at any time. Consent is not a one-off decision.

## The first song. The first movement. The first laugh.

We start with Queen – We Will Rock You. I show a simple sequence: shoulders twice, knees twice and clap twice. Once the client has it, we try varying the rhythm. Some people make a mistake, some lose track, and we often laugh together. If the harder sequence doesn’t work, we go back to the simpler one. It’s not about perfection. The person discovers that they can make a mistake and nothing bad happens.

## The music changes and the body learns new movements

Before the next songs, I show new movements. We work with pressing into the palms, pulling, alternating arm movements, rocking and coordination across the midline of the body. The client can copy me, but doesn’t have to keep up with everything. Various emotions may come up during the music. I watch for signs of overwhelm, panic, disorientation or physical discomfort. If it gets to be too much, we slow down or stop the intervention. Safety always comes before finishing a song.

## And then comes Happy

With Pharrell Williams – Happy, we move from structured to spontaneous movement. “Try noticing your body. What would it need right now?” Some people rock, others dance and some just sit. If the music isn’t pleasant, we can turn it down or switch it off. Deciding not to move is fine too.

## Minute twenty-one. The atmosphere changes.

Happy ends and Édith Piaf begins. I get the sheet ready and, if needed, help the client move into it. The pace changes. They no longer have to follow movement sequences; they can rest. Not everyone feels calm straight away after lying down, though. The client can just sit in the sheet, or lie on the floor with a blanket, or with a safely used weighted blanket. Some describe the sheet as a hug or an embrace. I don’t tell them what they should feel.

## Touch, rocking and quiet presence

Once the client has settled and agrees, I gently rock the sheet. Sometimes I place my hand only on the sheet, other times, as agreed, between the shoulder blades. I sit beside them, quietly humming or singing. If they have agreed to turning, I go slowly and watch how they respond. Sometimes it’s best to stay without movement for a while.

## What if tears come?

Sometimes crying, sadness, anger or tension come up. Other times nothing comes. If the client cries, I don’t automatically try to stop them, and I don’t explain what the tears mean. I stay with them and watch their orientation and their ability to cope with what they are experiencing. With panic, dissociation or marked discomfort, we slow down or stop both the movement and the music, and help them orient themselves or get out of the sheet. The aim is not to provoke the strongest possible emotions. Even the sentence “This is getting to be too much for me” is an important part of working with boundaries.

## When a person doesn’t need to talk

During the second phase we don’t have to talk. Sometimes quiet presence, music and gentle movement are enough. Bodily sensations, images or memories may come up. We don’t automatically treat them as accurate records of the past or as proof that trauma is being processed. Some people simply rest. That’s fine too.

## The last minutes. Coming back to yourself.

At the end, I gradually release the sheet from its twist. When the music ends, the client lies there a little longer. I invite them to notice their natural breath. Then, in our imagination, we trace the outline of the body from the big toe, through the feet, torso, arms and head. They gently move their fingers, open their eyes, look around and put their feet on the floor. I give them time.

## When is a person ready to leave the sheet?

I watch their orientation, communication, balance and physical comfort. If they feel weak or dizzy, I help them sit down in the armchair, offer water and we wait. Serious or persistent symptoms call for a medical assessment. The music has its end, but the person needs their own time to come back.

## So what actually happened during the therapy?

Back in the reception room, we rate anxiety, tension, safety, calm and energy again. I ask what the client felt, whether any emotions, images or memories came up, and what they would like to be different next time. Some talk for a long time, others say “It felt good” or “I didn’t feel anything.” I respect that too.

## What does the second HRV measurement show?

After about ten minutes of rest comes another five-minute HRV measurement. We compare the values before and after the session. But a single number doesn’t mean success or failure. Breathing, movement, sleep, the current state and the client’s own assessment matter too. In our pilot data collection, we track both physiological and subjective changes.

## What can the client expect after leaving?

Some leave relaxed, others tired or wanting to be alone. I recommend paying attention to sleep, energy and emotions, and perhaps writing down some notes. A marked worsening of one’s mental state is not a necessary part of therapy. Driving or doing demanding activities is best left until you are fully alert and oriented. If difficulties persist, contact a professional.

## Every next session can be different

The client may already know the movements and be better able to choose the lighting, volume or distance. Maybe for the first time they will say, “I don’t want this today.” After five sessions, we review together what the work has brought and the options for continuing, including a calmer regulation variant. Not everyone needs to continue, though.

## What still surprises me in first sessions?

Every first session is different. At the beginning I often see awkwardness. Then someone makes a mistake and we start laughing. The atmosphere gradually changes. Clients sometimes say they haven’t had this much fun in a long time. For many, the sheet becomes a favourite place to rest. Not because it heals trauma by itself, but because it gives them a pleasant space of their own.

## Come with me to the 1990s for a moment

I love remembering the Friday discos of the 1990s. After a whole week, we went out among people we felt good with. The music played, we danced, laughed and let the rhythm carry us away. We didn’t have to get the steps perfect. NRI is not a disco, and its therapeutic effects can’t be derived from a pleasant experience alone. But the memory captures the idea that movement doesn’t have to be a duty. It can be a joy. And rest doesn’t have to be a reward for performance.

## In closing

If you are getting ready for your first NRI and feel worried, I want to tell you one thing. You don’t have to prove anything. You don’t have to know how to dance, relax straight away or talk about painful experiences. You can laugh, rest, try things out or say that there’s something you don’t want today. You can change your decision at any time. The first NRI session is not a test of what you can do. It’s a chance to find out what you need, what suits you and at what pace you want to go. Even a moment filled with music, movement, acceptance and human closeness can have its place in your life.
