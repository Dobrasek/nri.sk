## Introduction

Music can reach us even without words. It can influence our emotions, attention, bodily activation and the urge to move. In NRI it is not just a backdrop: it helps create rhythm, predictability and space for sensing the body.

## When the music played, but my body couldn’t move

The idea of NRI began to take shape after a seminar where we were supposed to follow the music with our own movement. Back then, I could barely move. And yet, afterwards I felt great relief. At home I put together a playlist of songs that had accompanied me through important periods of my life. Little by little, small movements appeared, then bigger and more natural ones. From them, the movement sequences of NRI began to emerge. I realised that a person may need structure first in order to allow themselves freedom. Since childhood, I’ve been wired this way: if I don’t understand, I can’t feel. So I threw myself into studying the importance of rhythm, vibration and movement.

## Why isn’t it enough to say “Move however you feel”?

At the first session, a client often watches the therapist and monitors their own movements. If I asked some clients – people like me at the beginning, for example – to move however they felt, it could stir up insecurity and shame in them. That’s why I first offer clear movement sequences. Once they become more familiar, clients can pay more attention to what they are experiencing. Only then can the question come: “What does my body need right now?”

## Rhythm as an organiser of movement

Rhythm can help anticipate the timing of the next movement. This is related to auditory-motor synchronisation. In practice I see that some children coordinate their movements more easily and stay focused with music; for others, music can be distracting instead. If a client can’t keep up with the rhythm, we slow the movement down, break it into smaller parts and, if needed, use a demonstration.

## When movement stops being a task

With repetition, a movement sequence can gradually become automatic. A child who at first concentrates hard on every clap may later smile, talk or add a sway while moving. The goal is not a perfect performance, but gaining confidence and room for spontaneity.

## Music and emotions

I originally chose the songs based on an inner feeling, not on the lyrics. The same song stirred different emotions in me at different times. It can be similar for clients: one feels strength, another fear, a third relief. Music can also bring up autobiographical memories. An emotional reaction, however, is not on its own proof of trauma processing.

## When the body responds before words do

Sometimes a client notices pressure in the chest, a change in breathing, tears or bodily tension without any clear memory. After the session I ask about body sensations, emotions and any images. Even the answer “nothing special” is fine. The intensity of the experience is not a measure of success. Before the work, relevant medical and psychological risks need to be assessed, even though a detailed account of the trauma is not necessary.

## Why pressure, pulling, resistance and rocking?

Pressure and pulling provide proprioceptive information about the force and position of the limbs; rocking also engages vestibular perception. Movements across the midline of the body require coordination of both sides. Symbolically, resistance can stand for the right to say no, pressure for the ability to stand up for yourself, and free movement for freedom of choice. These meanings are not universal and must never be imposed on the client.

## Your own rhythm matters more than perfect attunement to the therapist

A shared rhythm can be a support, but the goal is to gradually sense your own needs better. The client decides on the lighting, volume, eye contact, the therapist’s distance and the intensity of movement. They have the right to decline an activity. Even a child who at first just lets themselves be led may in time begin to show initiative and joy in working together.

## When a person stops smiling all the time

After the first sessions, one client described feeling irritable, even though she was used to always being nice and smiling. Later she began allowing herself more disagreement and anger. In one session a memory came back of her mother, who, as she put it, smiled no matter what. Strong emotional experiencing followed, along with further work with body sensations. The client described relief and a readiness for change. This anonymised story is an individual experience, not proof of the effectiveness of any particular song.

## Predictability as a path to security

A fixed order of songs helps with learning the sequences and forming expectations. The client knows when more intense movement is coming and when things slow down. But if a song triggers an unpleasant reaction, we can turn it down, stop it or replace it. Predictability makes sense when it gives the client a sense of security and safety, not when it forces them to stay in a situation that is unpleasant or overwhelming for them. The goal of NRI is not to teach a person to endure discomfort at any cost, but to create conditions in which they can gradually recognise their needs, respect their own boundaries and gain trust in their abilities.

## What happens in the brain and nervous system?

- **Auditory-motor synchronisation:** rhythm helps time repeated movements.
- **Basal ganglia and cerebellum:** involved in timing, coordination, prediction and motor learning.
- **Emotional processing of music:** music can evoke emotions and autobiographical memories; the effect is individual.
- **Proprioception and the vestibular system:** pressure, resistance and rocking provide information about the position and movement of the body.
- **Predictability and learning:** repetition supports learning movement sequences and forming expectations.
- **Interpersonal synchronisation:** a shared rhythm can support coordination and social attunement, but the client doesn’t have to copy the therapist.
- **Autonomic regulation:** music and movement can change the level of activation, though not in the same way for everyone. These mechanisms do not on their own confirm the effectiveness of the whole NRI protocol.

## Conclusion

Music gives movement a temporal structure. Movement gives the body a concrete experience, and a safe relationship gives room for choice. NRI brings these elements together with the aim of supporting body awareness, personal initiative and the exploration of ways to regulate. What matters is not following the music perfectly, but gradually discovering your own rhythm.

## Professional and safety notice

NRI is a method still in development; the effectiveness of the complete protocol has not yet been sufficiently confirmed by high-quality controlled studies. The symbolic meanings of the movements are neither diagnostic signs nor proof of trauma processing. The client can decline or stop the movement or the music. In case of sensory overload, anxiety, dizziness or dissociation, the intensity must be adjusted or the activity stopped. Touch requires consent. With significant psychological, neurological or movement difficulties, the suitability of the intervention must be assessed individually. NRI does not replace indicated medical care.
